# Paper brief

## Audience

Economists and quantitatively literate researchers. The viewer should not need
to know the notation of the paper before watching.

## One-sentence contribution

The paper develops a dynamic sufficient statistic for the welfare effects of
labor-demand shocks and shows that economically diverse cities give workers
more local adjustment options, especially after adverse shocks.

## Economic mechanism

Workers occupy sector–occupation–city labor markets. After a shock they can
switch locally, move across cities, or leave employment. A diversified city
contains more nearby alternatives, so a worker can adjust with less disruption.

## Evidence

1. Local labor-demand shocks combine national sector–occupation growth with
   predetermined local exposure.
2. LPIV estimates trace within-city and across-city worker flows after positive
   and negative shocks across the HHI distribution.
3. Published Table 2 reports the first-order, second-order, and total discounted
   welfare effects.

## Welfare interpretation

The reported numbers are average welfare effects for workers in France
constructed from the realized French shock series. Positive and negative
realizations are averaged within HHI bins and evaluated separately. Effects
accumulate from 2006Q1–2019Q4 through horizon 20 with beta equal to 0.99 and
are measured relative to the baseline.

## Visual vocabulary

- Blue: estimated worker-flow responses and first-order effects.
- Green: diversity, local options, and the second-order component.
- Orange: concentration and adverse shocks.
- Cream: totals and main claims.

## Claims to exclude

- The positive and negative rows are not components of one national transition.
- The included city-year shock excerpt is not confidential worker microdata.
- The HHI used empirically is a proxy for the concentration object in the
  theoretical corollary.
- The welfare formula is a local approximation.
